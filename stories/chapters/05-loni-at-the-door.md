# Loni at the Door

Loni came home carrying the smell of cold earth.

It was the first thing Buxbrew noticed when the door opened: that clean, damp scent of the hills beyond Ironforge, caught in the folds of her sister's cloak. The second thing was the way Loni hesitated when she found the brewery crowded.

"You're standing in the doorway," Buxbrew called.

"I can see that."

"Come in, then."

Loni came in. Her short amber hair had been flattened by her hood, and a narrow line of dirt crossed one cheek. She carried her pack herself, though two people offered to take it. She thanked them and kept hold of it.

Bess found her before she had reached the table.

"There's my wandering girl."

Loni endured a fierce embrace with both feet planted. When Bess released her, she looked around the busy room as though considering the likelihood of escaping into the kitchen.

"Have you eaten?" Bess asked.

"A little."

"Not here, you haven't."

That settled supper.

Buxbrew made room for her at a side table, away from the loudest customers. Loni sat with her back to the wall and unfastened her cloak. There was no animal at her heels and no beast waiting outside for her whistle. She had always preferred the wild country on her own terms.

"Where've you been?" Buxbrew asked.

Loni tore a piece of bread.

"Walking."

"That narrows it down."

"In the hills."

"I'd have guessed that."

Loni ate the bread and said no more. Buxbrew could wait. She knew her sister well enough to understand that silence was not always an invitation to be filled.

Bess arrived with a plate of ribs and a second mug.

"Your sister's been asking after you," she told Loni.

"She asks after everyone."

"Not in the same way."

Buxbrew lifted her mug. "Cheers."

Loni answered with the smallest movement of her own.

For a while they ate. A game of dice began at the far table. Bess crossed the room to intervene in an argument about the value of a chipped token, and by the time she returned the argument had changed into a story about a lost fishing rod.

"Father been through?" Loni asked.

"Not lately," Buxbrew said.

Loni nodded, accepting the answer for what it was. Their father's tracks led to places he did not always describe, and there was little to gain from guessing.

After the second mug, Loni told Buxbrew about a path she had taken where the wind blew so hard she had to turn her face away to breathe. She showed her a scrape on one wrist, then refused to say how she had got it.

"Was it a fall?"

"No."

"A fight?"

"No."

"Did the mountain strike you out of spite?"

Loni gave her a look, but the corner of her mouth moved.

Buxbrew leaned forward. "I knew it."

"You're impossible."

"Only when you're here."

There was another round. Someone began singing, not very well. Loni glanced toward the sound and unexpectedly joined in on the next line. Buxbrew looked at her in delight. Loni told her not to make anything of it, which was a little too late.

Before long the sisters were laughing loudly enough for Bess to hear them from the kitchen.

"Keep your feet off the good chairs," their mother called.

Buxbrew looked at Loni.

Loni looked at Buxbrew.

"She didn't say anything about the tables," Buxbrew murmured.

"Don't."

But Loni was smiling now, and Buxbrew had already risen.

They did not stay on the table long. It was enough to make the men with the dice look up and laugh, and enough for Bess to shake her head while trying not to do the same.

Much later, when the room had thinned, Loni stood near the door with her cloak in her hands. Buxbrew passed her the pack she had set beneath the bench.

"There's a place to sleep," Buxbrew said.

"I know."

Loni lingered, looking from the warm tables to the narrow doorway.

Then she put the cloak down again.
